1.  Introduction 
 
1.1 Introduction 
 
The world is a dynamic place in which steady state is seldom reached: there is time dependence.  
All physicochemical processes employed by chemical engineers are similar in that they are 
constantly responding to changes.  Changes may be, for example, fluctuations in feed flow rate, 
temperature or composition, or they may be imposed by the operator to deal with a new product 
specification.  The response to a change is dynamic behaviour, which necessitates a means of 
control to ensure that process parameters are maintained within desired limits, or specifications are 
met.  The requirements of control systems have become much more demanding in recent years 
because of increased competition, stricter environmental and safety regulations, and also because 
of the increased complexity and integration of modern plants.  In large plants, such as oil refineries, 
thousands of variables will be monitored and controlled. 
 
Chemical engineers must be able to describe the dynamics of a process and then, based on an 
understanding of its behaviour, design the appropriate control system.  The design of a process 
plant must consider from the outset how the plant is to be controlled, as state-of-the-art control can 
never entirely change the effect of decisions introduced by process design: however good a driver 
you are, you can’t make a family saloon perform like a racing car. 
 
The broad aims of this course are to enable you to: 
 
• 
Analyse the dynamic behaviour of processes.  
• 
Design and analyse controllers and control systems for stability and control of quality. 
• 
Devise advanced control systems. 
• 
Devise control strategies for unit operations 
 
The course contains four main sections: 
 
Section 2 – Dynamics of Linear Systems  
 
We will review and extend the Process Dynamics part of CEBT IB Engineering Mathematics. 
 
Section 3 – Feedback Controller Design 
 
We will study feedback control of a single input/single output system, including proportional, 
integral and derivative control, frequency response, stability and controller tuning. 
 
Section 4 – Advanced Control Systems 
 
We will study aspects of advanced control systems, including cascade, feedforward and ratio 
control, and the control of nonlinear systems.

1.4  Feedforward Control 
 
A different approach to deal with disturbances in the inlet temperature T0, for example, is to 
measure it, and adjust Q accordingly. 
 
 
 
T0 is measured, and the information is fed forward to a model of the process.  The model is used to 
calculate the value of Q which will maintain T at its set point.  In contrast to feedback control, 
feedforward control allows control action to occur without there being an error in the controlled 
variable.  But for perfect control a perfect model is needed.  Feedforward control, and other 
advanced control techniques, will be discussed in Section 4. 
 
1.5 Control Objectives  
 
Control objectives fall into three main categories all of which need to be considered when 
designing the control system for a process plant: 
 
Safety – Control systems are one of the lines of defence employed to prevent safety and 
environmental constraints being breached but they are not solely relied upon as they can fail.  
Hence additional layers of higher integrity equipment incorporating shutdown logic with additional 
sensors and final elements are also incorporated in the design with a mechanical relief valve being 
the ultimate safety system. 
 
Operability – This term is used to describe the notion of being able to make the plant do what the 
operator wants rather than leaving it to its natural inclinations.  The majority of control loops are 
associated with operability and this objective is achieved in the design of the individual control 
loops.  Specific flow rates, temperatures, pressures and levels need to be set and maintained in 
order for the plant to perform.  
 
Profitability -  The top level of process control is the strategic control level and is concerned with 
achieving the desired production rate, product quality and energy efficiency.  This objective is 
achieved by placing the individual control loops in the correct part of the flowsheet and ensuring 
that there are no conflicts between individual controllers.  It can also often involve the use of 
advanced control systems that govern how control loops operate together. 
 
This course is primarily focused on the operability and profitability objectives.

1.6 Variables 
 
The controlled variable is the process variable being regulated by the system.  In a feedback 
controller the controlled variable is usually the measured variable.  Additional measured variables 
that are not necessarily controlled may be selected for monitoring the process.  In order to achieve 
the control objective the controlled variable must be matched with one or more variables that can 
be adjusted, the manipulated variables.  For example, the temperature controller on the reboiler of 
a distillation column can adjust the steam flow to regulate the temperature, and hence the 
composition, at the bottom of the column.

understand the process.  The starting point is writing the appropriate dynamic heat and material 
balances, as introduced in CEBT IB Heat and Mass Transfer and Engineering Mathematics; this 
section starts with a review. 
 
The advent of more powerful desktop computing means that dynamic process models can now be 
easily built using commercial software packages.

Process control is concerned with how the output variables from a system are affected by a 
change in the input variables.  A convenient way of modelling this behaviour is through the use of 
transfer functions and block diagrams.  As will be seen later, having the system represented in the 
s-domain of the Laplace transform allows us to gain a valuable insight into the system behaviour 
without determining the complete transient solution.   
 
An important constraint is that the set of differential equations must be linear.  If they are not, they 
can be linearised to give approximate results in the region of a steady state - as process control is 
generally interested in operation over a narrow range of process parameters, this is generally OK. 
 
This section introduces the transfer function which is a fundamental building block for all process 
control problems as it contains all the steady-state and dynamic information about a process in a 
compact form which is convenient to manipulate. 
 
For process control purposes systems can be categorised as: 
 
• 
Single Input- Single Output (SISO) – one input variable affects one output variable. 
• 
Multiple Input-Multiple Output (MIMO) – multiple input variables will affect multiple output 
variables.
